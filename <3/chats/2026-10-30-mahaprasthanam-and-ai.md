*Mahaprasthanam* is a powerful work to return to while thinking about AI. It holds together two feelings that can easily come apart: **excitement that another world is possible, and anger at what the existing world does to ordinary people.** That tension is a useful place to begin.

One small clarification: **మహాప్రస్థానం (*Mahāprasthānam*) is both a poetry collection and the title of its opening poem**, which you may recognize from *“Maro prapancham…”* Sri Sri—Srirangam Srinivasa Rao—wrote much of the collection during the 1930s; it appeared as a book in 1950. The title is commonly translated as *The Great Journey*; the opening poem also appears in English as *Forward March*. [MANUU’s introduction](https://manuu.edu.in/dde/sites/default/files/DDE/DDE-SelfLearnmaterial/11-Dec-2022/BA2ndSem/English-(Core-Paper).pdf)

**What was so important about it?**

Sri Sri helped shift the center of gravity of Telugu poetry toward workers, hunger, poverty, and the people whose suffering respectable society could overlook. He gave those lives the emotional scale and grandeur of epic poetry. His work became closely associated with progressive and Marxist literary culture, although its mixture of compassion, revolutionary imagination, and poetic experiment is richer than a political label alone suggests. [Uma Nuthakki’s essay in *Bhumika*](https://bhumika.org/archives/5856)

The opening gives you its characteristic energy:

> మరో ప్రపంచం  
> మరో ప్రపంచం  
> మరో ప్రపంచం పిలిచింది!

*Maro prapancham … pilichindi!* Roughly: **“Another world … has called!”** [Telugu text](https://telugu.pratilipi.com/story/maha-prasthanam-by-srirangam-srinivasa-rao-kvwto3dwz1dz)

Notice the emotional move: that other world already feels close enough to hear. Repetition turns an idea into a physical summons. You can feel why someone would remember the sound for decades, even when individual meanings become hazy.

There is a wonderful firsthand account by Telugu literary scholar **Velcheru Narayana Rao**: as a schoolboy, he encountered these poems around a workers’ procession and a bookshop, learned them through recitation and handwritten copies, and knew some by heart before the collection was printed. He also explains how the marching rhythm draws listeners into a collective experience. Your parents’ attachment belongs to a much larger history of people *hearing, remembering, and living with* this poetry. Their own story of discovering it would be worth hearing. [His essay, “రెండు శ్రీల కవి”](https://eemaata.com/em/issues/201001/1538.html?allinonepage=1)

**The AI connection**

My reading—not a claim about what Sri Sri would have thought of AI—is that his poetry gives us a way to interrogate promises of a new world.

- **What makes the new world better?** Greater productive capacity is one thing; freedom from hunger, humiliation, and exploitation is another achievement that people have to organize.
- **Whose work becomes invisible?** In *Desa Charitralu* (“Histories of Nations”), he directs attention away from celebrated rulers toward the laborers who built their monuments. Applied to AI, that invites questions about whose knowledge, creative work, and labor make the systems possible, and who receives the rewards. [Poem text in transliteration](https://sandeepreddy2019.wordpress.com/2013/03/17/maha-prasthanam-sree-sree/)
- **Who gets to shape the future?** The collective invitation in the title poem suggests people becoming participants in history. That makes a revealing comparison with any account of technological change that asks people simply to accept what arrives.

There’s also a tension worth keeping open: the exhilaration of marching toward the future can make its human costs harder to see. I would read the collection’s triumphant passages alongside its attention to suffering. Each complicates the other.

For an introduction, I’d begin with three poems:

1. **Mahaprasthanam** — the call toward another world; read it aloud.
2. **Jayabheri** — the individual’s contribution to collective transformation, with its memorable *“Nenu saitham”* (“I too”).
3. **Desa Charitralu** — history reconsidered through the people whose work and suffering it leaves unnamed.

The first gives you momentum; the second, a place within it; the third, questions to ask about where that momentum leads.

When you say it feels relevant to AI, is it mostly **the sense of another world approaching**, or **the worry about what happens to people’s work and dignity**?
