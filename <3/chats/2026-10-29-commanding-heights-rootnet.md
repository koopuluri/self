Yes—on extreme concentration, and on risk becoming **more shared across the system**. But the strongest challenge to ROOTNET is this: **advancing the frontier and dispersing power are separate achievements. Your draft needs a stronger mechanism connecting them.**

The essay makes three relevant arguments:

| Your question | What the authors argue |
|---|---|
| **Does productivity concentrate?** | A few exceptional operators command enormous output and become “capitalized cash flows.” This is a concentration thesis, not an empirically established power-law distribution. [“No-Collar Labor”](https://commanding-heights.com/) |
| **What world follows?** | Cheap intelligence coexists with concentrated ownership, weakened labor, and increasingly private access to security, education, and protection. Abundance can coexist with exclusion. [“The Private Commons”](https://commanding-heights.com/) |
| **Does risk grow everywhere?** | Apparently different investments increasingly depend on the same AI expansion. Their failures become correlated. That is different from demonstrating that every worthwhile pursuit becomes harder or riskier. [“The Totalization”](https://commanding-heights.com/) |

Reading your draft against that, these are the overlaps and challenges I would take seriously. The implications for ROOTNET below are my analysis.

1. **Your strongest overlap: the person may outlast the investable idea.**

   If particular projects become obsolete faster, backing someone’s capacity to discover and pursue successive opportunities becomes more compelling. Personal stock could let supporters survive the failure of the first idea alongside the person they backed.

   Your strongest argument here is: **the unit of backing should persist across changes in the work.** That is more precise than saying everything gets riskier. It explains why company equity might be insufficient.

2. **Your biggest disagreement: progress can strengthen the incumbent.**

   You propose: discovery → new opportunities → competition → dispersed power. The essay’s countermechanism is that expanding activity reinforces whoever controls its indispensable channels. [“The Commanding Heights”](https://commanding-heights.com/)

   Suppose ROOTNET funds a breakthrough whose commercialization depends on an incumbent’s infrastructure, distribution, or permission. The breakthrough succeeds; the incumbent becomes stronger.

   Your missing distinction is between **discoveries that expand economic activity** and **discoveries that create independent centers of economic power**. ROOTNET needs to explain how its members retain enough ownership, access, and autonomy to accomplish the second.

3. **You may be locating the concentration at the wrong layer.**

   The authors expect durable gains to migrate beyond model production toward distribution, transactions, and politically sustained control. [“Overture”](https://commanding-heights.com/)

   This challenges your opening claim that frontier labs will dominate value creation and your implied link between that dominance and stagnation. A world could have fiercely competitive labs, rapidly advancing science, and extremely concentrated economic power elsewhere.

   **Even permanently accelerating progress would not, by itself, disprove the concentration problem.** ROOTNET’s rationale should survive that possibility.

4. **A harder frontier does not imply that worthwhile work becomes harder.**

   Your draft moves between three different thresholds: discovering something unknown, doing something useful, and building something defensible.

   AI could raise the threshold for originality while lowering the cost of experimentation. A neglected customer problem can remain worth solving even when an existing model knows how. Conversely, something can become easier to build but harder to earn from.

   I would replace the universal claim with a narrower hypothesis: **AI may shorten the useful life of an advantage faster than it reduces the cost of discovering the next one.** That directly supports financing people through repeated reinvention. Whether the effect is exponential remains something to establish.

5. **ROOTNET need not flatten productivity to broaden prosperity.**

   This is potentially your most consequential insight. Even if a few people generate most monetizable outcomes, shared ownership can distribute those outcomes much more broadly.

   You have two distinct mechanisms: backing overlooked people can expand who gets to contribute; network ownership can share gains even when contributions remain unequal. Either could matter without proving the other.

   There is also a tension worth preserving: measured individual output may depend on mentors, peers, and institutions whose contributions remain invisible. Your network mechanism challenges the assumption that the apparent superstar produced the outcome alone.

   **Concentrated outcomes do not establish concentrated deservingness.**

6. **Pooling risk works only where failures differ.**

   Personal stocks can spread exposure across someone’s projects; network stocks can spread it across people. Neither automatically protects against a common shock.

   A hundred members depending on the same platform, funding market, or technological advantage may fail together. Their reciprocal ownership could then transmit distress rather than provide support.

   For ROOTNET, distinguish **the risk of an unsuccessful experiment** from **the risk that the entire network loses its means of sustaining experiments**. The latter requires resources that remain available during shared adversity. More members alone do not provide that.

7. **Universal basic equity needs a bridge from ownership to freedom.**

   Your draft says regular share issuance gives ignored members freedom to pursue difficult work. But shares finance that freedom only when they provide access to actual resources—through distributions, purchases by others, or another funding mechanism.

   If everyone receives new shares in proportion to existing ownership, relative ownership does not change. Equal grants can redistribute ownership, but issuance itself does not create additional resources.

   There is also a boundary question: universal *among whom*? A selective network can share prosperity internally while increasing exclusion externally. Admission, control, and members’ ability to leave therefore bear directly on your stated mission.

The version of your thesis I find strongest is: **ROOTNET gives people the resources to keep searching before their potential is legible, lets supporters participate across successive pursuits, and gives a wider community ownership in the rare successes.** That case remains compelling even if progress does not inherently decentralize power—and makes clear what ROOTNET must accomplish beyond simply accelerating it.
