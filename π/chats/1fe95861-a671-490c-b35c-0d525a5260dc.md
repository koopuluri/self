> π: The line you were remembering in beat 1394 is from the **Kaṭha Upaniṣad 1.3.14**. And there’s a small difference between the verse and your memory of it that I think makes *both* more interesting.
> 
> > uttiṣṭhata jāgrata prāpya varān nibodhata |  
> > kṣurasya dhārā niśitā duratyayā  
> > durgaṃ pathas tat kavayo vadanti ||
> 
> A close translation:
> 
> > Rise! Awake! Approach the excellent teachers and learn.  
> > A razor’s edge is sharp, difficult to cross;  
> > hard to travel is that path, the sages say.
> 
> Your phrase was *“the path is as narrow as a razor’s edge.”* The Sanskrit emphasizes **sharpness and difficulty of passage**, not explicitly narrowness. And your most striking thought—*“if you crave reaching the end of the razor you lose the awareness of the moment you require to not fall off where you are”*—isn’t stated in the verse. That’s your contribution to the image: you’ve given an account of **why looking too hard at the destination can make the path unwalkable**.
> 
> ### What path is the Upaniṣad talking about?
> 
> The teacher is **Yama, Death himself**. A boy named Naciketas asks him what happens to a person after death: some say the person still exists, others say not. Yama offers him long life, wealth, music, descendants—almost anything rather than answer. Naciketas declines. Even the longest life ends; what could Death give him that would settle the question *about* death?
> 
> So the razor isn’t originally an image for the difficulty of achieving any ambitious goal. It’s about coming to know the **Self (ātman)**. Earlier, Yama pictures the body as a chariot, the senses as horses, the mind as reins, and the discerning intellect as charioteer. Immediately before the razor verse, the movement turns inward: draw speech into mind, mind into the knowing self, and ultimately into the peaceful Self. Immediately *after* it, Yama describes what is to be known as soundless, touchless, formless, imperishable.
> 
> That placement matters. **“Wake up!” is followed by a task that force alone cannot accomplish.** The student needs urgency—Naciketas won’t be bought off or distracted—but what he’s trying to discern is subtler than the things he’s accustomed to chasing. That feels close to your distinction between *having an objective* and *craving it*.
> 
> ### The Western book: W. Somerset Maugham’s *The Razor’s Edge*
> 
> Maugham took the title of his 1944 novel **directly** from this verse. Its epigraph reads:
> 
> > The sharp edge of a razor is difficult to pass over; thus the wise say the path to Salvation is hard.
> 
> Notice what that rendering adds: **“to Salvation.”** The Sanskrit line itself says *“that path”*; you learn which path from the surrounding dialogue.
> 
> Maugham also carries over something deeper than the title. His protagonist, **Larry Darrell**, survives the First World War after another airman dies saving him. Back home, he’s offered what everyone around him considers a good life: marriage, a career, security. He can’t take its goodness for granted anymore. His search eventually takes him to India and to Vedānta. The echo of Naciketas is especially lovely: **both encounter death, and afterward the ordinary rewards aren’t exactly bad; they’re answers to a different question.**
> 
> ### Four neighboring images, each with a different insight
> 
> - **The Buddha’s lute string — [Soṇa Sutta, AN 6.55](https://suttacentral.net/an6.55/en/sujato).** Soṇa has been striving so fiercely in walking meditation that his feet bleed, yet he’s getting nowhere. The Buddha asks him about something he already knows: when he played the lute, did it sound right with the strings too tight? No. Too loose? No. This may be the closest parallel to your *“sufficiently balanced.”* The point isn’t to split the difference between caring and not caring. **You listen for the tension at which the instrument can actually play.**
> 
> - **The fruit of action — *Bhagavad Gītā* 2.47–48.** Krishna tells Arjuna that action is his province, not its fruits—but immediately warns him **not to become attached to inaction either**. That second half is crucial. Letting go of the result is not a reason to step off the razor and sit down. Arjuna still has to act. Of all these passages, this is the closest to your distinction between *direction* and *craving*: you can give yourself fully to what you’re doing without demanding that the future pay you back.
> 
> - **The very narrow bridge — [Rabbi Nachman of Breslov, *Likutei Moharan* II.48](https://www.sefaria.org/Likutei_Moharan%2C_Part_II.48).** The famous song says the whole world is a very narrow bridge and the main thing is not to be afraid. The original teaching has a more delicate formulation: a person must cross a very, very narrow bridge, and the essential thing is **not to frighten oneself**. That’s different from commanding yourself to feel no fear. The bridge is narrow enough already; you needn’t also rehearse the fall. This is the **anxiety** counterpart to your observation about craving.
> 
> - **Cook Ding’s blade — *Zhuangzi*, chapter 3, “Nourishing Life.”** Here the image is almost inverted. Instead of a person trying to travel along a blade, **the blade itself has to find a path**. An expert cook guides his knife through the spaces in an ox’s joints rather than hacking through resistance. My favorite detail is what happens at a difficult spot: despite all his skill, he becomes attentive, fixes his gaze, slows down, and moves the knife very slightly. Mastery hasn’t made attention unnecessary. It has taught him **when to slow down enough to see**.
> 
> If I were to put two of these beside your beat, I’d choose **the Kaṭha for the image and Soṇa for the practice**. The razor tells you why attention matters. The lute tells you how to respond when you notice you’ve lost it: not by trying harder as a matter of principle, nor by letting go as a matter of principle. **Listen to the note you’re actually getting.**
