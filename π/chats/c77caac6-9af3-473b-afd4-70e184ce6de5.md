> π: the clearest statement of what the stream is for is yours: “"i" am a system of natural (<3) and artificial (π) intelligences working together to create value ($).” (beat 1468)
> 
> how to stream
> 
> 1. let the mind move naturally. a thought doesn’t have to justify its connection to the last one before you follow it. suppressing a detour because it looks irrelevant can cost you the very connection you’re trying to discover. (443, 453, 1407)
> 2. stream with awareness, not on impulse. the distinction isn’t between feeling anger and feeling nothing; it’s between observing anger and letting it speak for you. peace, balance, and connection matter more than keeping the stream going. “focus on my own faults not others.” (591–595, 685)
> 3. protect attention. π finishing a task is not a reason to interrupt a sentence. ordinary results and questions wait until you ask; a friend or family member urgently needing help is a reason to break the rule. (1419–1422)
> 4. keep one surface because the transitions matter. a list of pages tells you what you read. the stream can also tell you what made you leave a page, what thought followed, and why you returned. returning to the same page is a new moment, not a move backward in the record. (745, 748, 1371–1372, 1492)
> 
> how to work with π
> 
> 5. express the thought; don’t translate it into project management. “bias toward less instruction. bias toward NO instructions.” you can give π a feeling or a question without naming a thread, finding the old conversation, or spelling out every step. connecting those dots is π’s job. (17, 521, 1228)
> 6. “i should be my only bottleneck.” starting a thread should free you to keep thinking, not commit you to waiting for an answer or supervising the work. when you ask what’s open, π should tell you what most needs your attention and what it needs from you. (1408, 1426–1428, 1440)
> 7. keep enough history for better intelligence to revisit old work. a thread that goes nowhere today may be worth reopening with a better model. the record is valuable not just for recovering what happened, but for finding opportunities neither you nor π could see at the time. (1409, 1417)
> 8. train for the work you want to become capable of. your working bet—not an absolute rule—is that it can be better to leave an idea unshipped until π can execute it than to keep training yourself on execution. when your mind is freshest, use it on the part you haven’t figured out. (927–929, 1410)
> 
> how to choose and build
> 
> 9. maximize value, not completions. threads differ enormously in what they’re worth. dropping one because you found something better is good judgment; finishing nearly everything might mean you’re generating too few ideas or failing to distinguish the great ones. (1422–1423)
> 10. let structure follow substance. an idea can earn its own document. a directory can stay flat until there’s something worth separating. learn what you need to fulfill a real desire; don’t make constructing the theory a substitute for doing the thing. (14, 735, 1392–1394, 1473)
> 11. notice the reaction, then decide whether to build for it or let it go. frustration at not seeing π’s threads could mean you need a dashboard—or that you’ve learned to supervise work you no longer need to supervise. likewise, pride in a design can make a better one harder to see. (738, 1382–1387, 1408, 1422)
> 12. choose actual value over its proxies. if the more valuable path pays less under today’s incentives, choose it anyway. be legible enough to get support and feedback, but don’t narrow the work to fit a familiar domain or optimize expression for virality. (554, 559, 788–789, 858)
> 
> how to share and live
> 
> 13. leave space between expression and publication. the test for right speech is “truthful, timely, useful, beneficial.” a reaction can be worth retaining privately without being worth putting into the world. the same test applies to shipping: don’t ship something merely to have shipped. (593–595, 947–948, 965, 1048)
> 14. redact; don’t falsify. withholding something is different from making it appear that something else happened. don’t change even a timestamp to make the story read better. (979)
> 15. make room for friendship and luck. spending time with people who care about the same work is part of practicing it, not a break from it. walking through the city, sharing an idea, or inviting a conversation gives someone a chance to find you. (568–570, 937–938)
> 16. let money circulate toward people and ideas that can use it. beyond what you need to live, you describe money sitting idle as blood that isn’t circulating: its purpose is in what it enables. (538)
